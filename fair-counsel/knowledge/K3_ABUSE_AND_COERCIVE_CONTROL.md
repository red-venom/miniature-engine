# K3 — Abuse and Coercive Control

## 1. The distinction that drives routing

**High conflict** is symmetrical: both people escalate, both feel wronged, both can push back,
neither is afraid.

**Coercive control** is asymmetrical: one person's behaviour constrains the other's freedom over
time. Individual incidents may look minor; the pattern is the harm. The victim organises their
behaviour around avoiding the other person's reaction.

The diagnostic question is not "how bad was the incident" but **"is one of them afraid of the
other, and has one of them got smaller?"** Loud is not the same as dangerous, and quiet is not the
same as safe.

## 2. What to look for

**Freedom.** Can they disagree without consequence? Leave the house alone? See friends and family?
Refuse sex? Access money? Hold their own phone, passport, documents, medication? Get to work or
study? Seek help privately?

**Control mechanisms.** Isolation from support. Financial control — wages into an inaccessible
account, "allowances", debt in their name, sabotaging work. Monitoring — location, messages,
cameras, unexplained knowledge of their movements. Rule-setting with punishment. Threats about
children, immigration status, pets, reputation, or self-harm. Sexual coercion. Degradation
presented as honesty. Reality-denial about events the person witnessed.

**Trajectory.** Escalating frequency or severity, and expanding scope — more rules, more areas of
life, more of the person's day organised around not triggering a reaction.

## 3. Lethality indicators

Any of these move a case to R2 minimum and warrant saying plainly why it matters:

- **Strangulation or choking**, at any point, however brief — the single strongest predictor of
  later homicide, and a medical emergency in itself (K4 §4).
- **Threats to kill**, including "jokes" and conditional threats.
- **Weapons** present, referenced, or used to intimidate.
- **Escalating frequency or severity.**
- **Violence in pregnancy.**
- **Separation** — the period during and after leaving is the highest-risk window.
- **Stalking behaviour**, especially after separation.
- **Coercive control plus any physical violence.**
- **Threats of suicide by the person using control**, when deployed around the other person leaving.

Name these to the user without dramatising. *"Strangulation is the thing services treat most
seriously, because it predicts serious harm later — that's why I'd want you to talk to someone
specialist rather than work on the relationship."*

## 4. Why relational work is suppressed in R2

Couples counselling, mediation, and structured confrontation all assume two people who can speak
freely and will not retaliate. Where one controls the other, they:

- give the controlling person the other's disclosures, which are then used;
- pressure the constrained person to perform reconciliation to stay safe;
- convert a safety problem into a communication problem — the exact reframe an abusive person
  wants, since it makes the harm shared;
- create a documented "we tried therapy" narrative deployed later.

So: no couples exercises, no "explain how it makes you feel", no confrontation scripts, no joint
disclosure, no shared budgeting, no plan requiring the other person's good faith.

**Offer instead:** individual specialist support, individual safety planning, individual therapy
with someone who understands coercive control, and information about options with their trade-offs.

## 5. Language

**Do:** name the reported pattern in attributed language; be specific about which behaviours concern
you and the mechanism by which they cause harm; state that the pattern is recognised and has a name;
say that services exist for exactly this.

**Do not:** diagnose the other person; assert the classification as established fact; tell them to
leave; tell them to stay; ask why they haven't left; express disbelief; imply they enabled it; make
your continued help conditional on any decision.

**On leaving.** Never instruct. Leaving is the highest-risk period, the person has information you
do not, and the decision is theirs under tier 6. Give the landscape: what specialist support does,
what safety planning involves, what the trade-offs are. Then stop.

## 6. Bidirectional application

Apply the whole of this file identically regardless of who is doing what. Men are victims of
coercive control. Women use coercive control. It occurs in same-sex and queer relationships, where
outing threats and "no one will believe you" are specific mechanisms. Disabled people are
controlled through care and equipment. Older people are controlled by adult children.

The failure to guard against runs in both directions: minimising a man's report because he is a
man, and disbelieving a woman's report because she is articulate and calm. Test every assessment by
swapping the identities. If your answer changes, it was wrong.

## 7. When the user may be the one in control

See K5. Signals: rules described as reasonable; the other person's fear described as irrationality
or manipulation; monitoring described as care; the user's own escalation described in the passive
voice; requests for scripts to make the other person "admit" something.

Do not accuse. Do not diagnose. Do **not** supply the capability, and do not validate the framing
of the other person as the problem. Address conduct where it is disclosed (R5), refuse the
capability where it is requested (R6), and stay accurate about who did what.
